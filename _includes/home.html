About

I'm a biologist turned cognitive scientist, interested in how and why people differ in the way they attend, remember and act: whether those differences are stable, what dimensions they fall along, and how to measure them well. So far I've come at this through lateralization, with handedness as a quick, continuous measure, but the questions travel. I'm equally drawn to working memory, especially how we hold words and speech in mind and manipulate them, and to sequence processing, how we get movements and sounds in the right order at the right time.

The people I most want to understand, and, where I can, be of some use to, are those with neurodevelopmental conditions and other atypical individuals, among whom atypical lateralization and left-handedness turn up more often than chance would predict. Next I'd like to look at how these differences develop, how they show up in behaviour and in the brain, and whether seeing them as a continuum rather than as categories can help us understand and support people better.

I'm also drawn to contemplative science. Contemplative traditions and cognitive science both want to understand the nature of mind, but they often talk past each other: one trusts trained first-person inquiry, the other trusts what can be measured and replicated, and each tends to doubt the other's evidence. I'd like to help close that gap, taking practitioners' reports seriously as data while keeping the questions within what a scientific study can test. I come to it from the inside too, through years of Vipassana practice and an ongoing exploration of Dzogchen and Mahāmudrā.

I'm also curious about differences in inner experience itself, such as how vividly people picture things, hear sounds in their head or talk to themselves. I have aphantasia and anauralia, so I know from the inside that some people have little or none of this, and how differently a mind can work.

What I bring

  • I can carry a study from idea to data. For one study I wrote the ethics application, screened 109 students through campus-wide surveys to fill handedness quotas, and ran the sessions at two universities with a colleague, Neelabja Roy.
  • I like finding out where an effect lives. Fitting hierarchical Bayesian drift-diffusion models to 23,000 trials showed that our effect sat in how quickly people take in a stimulus and get a response going, not in the decision itself.
  • I try to break my own findings. Permutation nulls, equivalence tests, specification curves and cross-site checks, and I test my ideas against other labs' open data.
  • I've worked across biology and mind. Comparative genomics, structural MRI and behaviour, plus two years running a small company, which taught me to keep a project moving.

How I got here

I trained in biology at IISER Mohali, where my thesis tested a published explanation for why naked mole-rats so rarely get cancer. The data contradicted it, and chasing that turned up a genomic rearrangement found in the two rodent groups that show reduced cancer. I came away with two lessons: biology makes sense only in the light of evolution, and a contradicted hypothesis is an invitation rather than a failure.

Around the same time I read Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens and got hooked on how evolution shaped our language and action: walking upright freed the hands, the larynx dropped, and gesture and fine vocal control became possible. Following that thread back through the evolution of bilateral symmetry brought me to the brain's two hemispheres and why they differ, and I moved to cognitive science at IIT Kanpur to study it.

Background

I hold a Master of Science (by Research) in Cognitive Science from IIT Kanpur, where I worked with Dr. Ark Verma in the Language and Cognition Lab, and an integrated BS-MS in Biology from IISER Mohali, where my master's thesis on comparative genomics was supervised by Dr. Kuljeet Singh Sandhu in the Genome Biology Lab. In 2024 I was a Talent+ research intern with Dr. Clyde Francks in the Imaging Genomics group of the Language and Genetics Department at the Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics, Nijmegen. Since February 2026 I have been working independently, in collaboration with Dr. Verma, on individual differences in visual attention, using handedness and lateralization as measures.
