About

I'm a biologist turned cognitive scientist, interested in individual variation in behaviour and experience: how and why people vary in the way they perceive, attend and act, and in how their minds seem from the inside. I want to know whether these variations are stable, what dimensions they fall along, and how to measure them well. I come at these questions along two main lines, lateralization and inner experience, and a third, contemplative science, that grows out of my own practice.

Lateralization, how the two halves of the brain divide up their work, is where I started. Handedness is my way in: it is quick to measure, and it tells you more than you'd expect once you treat it as a scale rather than splitting people into left- and right-handers. It also matters beyond the lab. Atypical lateralization and left-handedness turn up more often than chance would predict in neurodevelopmental conditions, and people with these conditions, and other atypical individuals, are the ones I most want to understand and, where I can, be of some use to.

Inner experience is the second line: how vividly people picture things, hear sounds in their head or talk to themselves. Here too the variation is large, yet it is usually measured only by asking people. I have aphantasia and anauralia, so I cannot picture images or hear sounds in my mind, but I do talk to myself, in words I say inwardly without hearing them. Questionnaires would count me as having no inner voice. That gap, between what people report and what their minds actually do, is something I would like to measure, by pairing careful reports with behavioural and physiological measures.

The two lines meet in speech. Language and the precise ordering of movements are among the most strongly lateralized functions of the brain, which is why I'm drawn to working memory, especially how we hold words in mind, and to sequence processing, how we get movements and sounds in the right order at the right time. Variation in lateralization should show up in both, as it does in the attention measures of my current work. The same speech system also carries inner speech: we hold words in mind largely by saying them silently, so people who talk to themselves less may remember and order things differently. In each case I'd like to look at how this variation develops, how it shows up in behaviour and in the brain, and whether seeing it as a continuum rather than as categories can help us understand and support people better.

I'm also drawn to contemplative science. Contemplative traditions and cognitive science both want to understand the nature of mind, but they often talk past each other: one trusts trained first-person inquiry, the other trusts what can be measured and replicated, and each tends to doubt the other's evidence. I'd like to help close that gap, taking practitioners' reports seriously as data while keeping the questions within what a scientific study can test. It connects naturally with inner experience, since meditation trains the very attention to one's own mind that reports of experience depend on. I come to it from the inside too, through years of Vipassana practice and an ongoing exploration of Dzogchen and Mahāmudrā.

What I bring

  • From idea to data. For one study I wrote the ethics application, screened 109 students to fill handedness quotas, and ran the sessions at two universities with a colleague, Neelabja Roy.
  • Modelling. Hierarchical Bayesian drift-diffusion models of 23,000 trials showed that our effect lay in how quickly people take in a stimulus and start a response, not in the decision itself.
  • Robustness. I check findings with permutation nulls, equivalence tests, specification curves and cross-site comparisons, and test my ideas against other labs' open data.

From genomes to minds

I trained in biology at IISER Mohali, where my thesis tested a published explanation for why naked mole-rats so rarely get cancer. The data contradicted it, and following that up turned up a genomic rearrangement shared by the two rodent groups that resist cancer; it taught me that a contradicted hypothesis is an invitation rather than a failure. Evolution then drew me to how the hands and the voice came under fine control, and from there to the brain's two hemispheres and why they differ, which I moved to IIT Kanpur to study. Inner experience came later, from discovering that I have aphantasia and anauralia, and from years of meditation that made it hard to ignore how differently minds can work from the inside.

Background

I hold a Master of Science (by Research) in Cognitive Science from IIT Kanpur, where I worked with Dr. Ark Verma in the Language and Cognition Lab, and an integrated BS-MS in Biology from IISER Mohali, where my master's thesis on comparative genomics was supervised by Dr. Kuljeet Singh Sandhu in the Genome Biology Lab. In 2024 I was a Talent+ research intern with Dr. Clyde Francks in the Imaging Genomics group of the Language and Genetics Department at the Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics, Nijmegen. Since February 2026 I have been working independently, in collaboration with Dr. Verma, on individual variation in visual attention, using handedness and lateralization as measures.
