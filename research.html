Handedness and the lateralization of visuospatial attention

Current

Independent researcher, with Dr. Ark Verma (IIT Kanpur) · Feb. 2026 – present

In 102 people spread across the handedness spectrum, where someone sits on the left–right handedness scale predicted how their two hemispheres shared out global–local and face processing, though not simpler visual features like orientation. Modelling their response times placed the effect in the time spent taking in the stimulus and getting a response under way, rather than in the decision itself.

A four-experiment divided visual half-field study (orientation and spatial frequency, global–local form, faces, words) in 102 participants (109 screened) recruited across IIT Kanpur and NIT Calicut, reported preliminarily at CogSci 2023 and now carried through to completion. Hierarchical Bayesian drift-diffusion modelling of 23,273 trials (brms/Stan) localises the visual-field × handedness interaction to nondecision time, with robustness checked by permutation, specification-curve, equivalence and cross-site heterogeneity analyses.

Disorder-associated brain structural asymmetry

2024

Talent+ Research Intern, Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics, Nijmegen · Sep. – Nov. 2024 · Supervisors: Dr. Clyde Francks and Dr. Meng-Yun Wang

Several neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions come with small shifts in how the left and right sides of the brain differ in structure. This project asked how those shifts line up with other features of brain biology.

Applied multivariate statistics, including principal component analysis and canonical correlation analysis, to disorder-associated measures of brain structural asymmetry, with cortical and subcortical measures extracted from structural MRI using FreeSurfer. Presented as a seminar talk at the Imaging Genomics Group Meeting.

Handedness continuum and lateralized cognitive functions

2019 – 2021

Master's thesis, IIT Kanpur · Supervisor: Dr. Ark Verma

In 39 people, where someone sat on the left–right handedness scale changed which side of the brain did more of the work in recognising tools and symmetry, and by how much. For action words it changed only how much.

Three divided visual half-field experiments — tool recognition, action-word processing and symmetry recognition — in 39 participants sampled across the full handedness continuum, testing whether the degree of handedness predicts hemispheric lateralization. Handedness affected both the degree and the direction of lateralization in tool and symmetry processing, but only the degree in action-word processing, suggesting that handedness has differential effects on the lateralization of low- and high-level processes.

Genomic rearrangement and cancer resistance in rodents

2017 – 2018

BS-MS thesis, IISER Mohali · Supervisor: Dr. Kuljeet Singh Sandhu

Naked mole-rats rarely get cancer. A popular explanation for this didn't hold up, but comparing genomes across mammals turned up a large DNA rearrangement shared only by the two groups of rodents that resist cancer.

Comparative genomic analysis across mammals uncovered a large rearrangement in the naked mole-rat genome that splits a tumour-suppressor gene cluster relative to humans. The convergent rearrangement is confined to two rodent suborders that also show reduced oncogenesis, bearing on the exceptional cancer resistance of those species.

Research directions

Underneath everything I have worked on is one question: why do people differ in how they attend, act and put things in order, and what do those differences mean for their lives? Lateralization has been my way into it so far, but it is one lens among several, and I am glad to come at the same question from other angles. These are the broad lines I would like to explore. How I pursue them would depend on the lab and the people I end up working with.

  1. Individual differences in attention and action

    Why people differ in how they focus attention, plan and control movement and process sequences, and how these abilities develop and relate to one another. Handedness and hemispheric asymmetry are useful handles on this, but not the only ones.

  2. Connecting behaviour and the brain

    Linking what people do in carefully designed tasks with how their brains are organised and how they work, ideally in the same individuals.

  3. Atypical development and lived experience

    Understanding the variation within neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions, and among other atypical individuals, as a continuum rather than as a set of categories, with an eye to what it means for the people themselves and how they might be better supported.

Along the way I would like to keep pairing well-designed behavioural experiments with computational modelling, and to pick up new methods, whether neuroimaging, developmental or clinical, wherever the question needs them.
